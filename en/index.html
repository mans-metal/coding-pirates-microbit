Meet the micro:bit

A tiny computer you can program yourself. It fits in your hand and it's packed with lights, buttons and sensors.

1. What is a micro:bit?

A tiny computer

It follows the instructions (code) that you write.

It can sense

Buttons, tilting, shaking, direction, temperature and light.

It can show

Pictures, numbers and words on its 25 little lights.

It can talk

It sends messages to other micro:bits by radio.

2. The front of the board

  1. USB port (on the back, top edge): plug a cable in here to send your code to the micro:bit and give it power.
  2. Buttons A and B: press them to make things happen. Pressing A and B together counts as a third button!
  3. LED grid: 25 red lights in 5 rows of 5. You control each one.
  4. Gold pins: the micro:bit's "fingertips". Connect wires and crocodile clips here.

What are the gold pins for?

Think of the pins as the micro:bit's fingertips. A pin can send a message out (switch a light on) or listen for one coming in (is a button pressed?). There are 25 gold strips. The five with big rings (0, 1, 2, 3V and GND) are the easy ones to clip onto.

Why is there a Ground?

Electricity only flows in a loop. Think of a pump pushing water through a pipe, round a little water wheel and back to the pump. The micro:bit is the pump: power goes out through 3V, through your part (a light, say) and back in through GND. If there's a gap in the loop, nothing moves and the light stays off. GND is short for "ground", a name borrowed from early telegraph lines, which really did use the earth as the way back. Your micro:bit isn't connected to the earth, so for us it just means "the way back". Kit parts also have a third wire, Signal, which carries the message ("switch on!").

3. The LED grid: finding your way around

Every light has an address made of two numbers: x (across) and y (down). We write it (x, y). Counting starts at 0, not 1!

  • x = how far across (left → right)
  • y = how far down (top → bottom)
  • (0, 0) is the top-left light.
  • (4, 4) is the bottom-right light.
  • (2, 2) is the middle light.
Quick quiz: Which light is at (4, 0)?   Which is at (0, 4)? Remember: x comes first, then y.

4. The back of the board

  1. USB port: plug in the cable to load code and give power.
  2. Reset button: restarts your program.
  3. Battery socket: plug in a battery pack to go cordless.
  4. Processor: the brain chip that follows your code.
  5. Pin rings: the gold rings you clip onto, the same pins as on the front.

Tiny sensor chips on the back do two jobs: the compass knows which way is north, and the accelerometer feels tilting, shaking and falling.

There are two versions of the micro:bit. The V1 has no speaker and no microphone. The newer V2 has both, plus a gold touch logo above the lights, so look for the gold oval to tell them apart. Both can talk by Bluetooth and radio. Blocks that only work on a V2 are marked V2 only in the lessons. Your kit's buzzer makes sound on either version.

5. How do you tell it what to do?

You write a program, then send it to the micro:bit. The website makecode.microbit.org is free and works in your browser. There is nothing to install, and you don't need to sign in: just start a New Project.

Blocks are the easiest way to start: snap them together, like in Scratch. You can also write the same program as Python or JavaScript text.

  1. Blocks / JavaScript: switch how you write your code (Python is in the menu).
  2. The micro:bit on screen: test your program here first.
  3. Block types: pick blocks from these colourful groups.
  4. Workspace: snap your blocks together here.
  5. Download: send your program to the real micro:bit.
  1. Go to makecode.microbit.org and start a new project.
  2. Build your program from blocks.
  3. Try it on the on-screen micro:bit.
  4. Connect your micro:bit with the USB cable and download. If it doesn't go straight on, copy the file onto the drive called MICROBIT.

7. The breakout board

The micro:bit's gold strips are tiny and hard to clip onto. The breakout board fixes that: the micro:bit slots into it, and most pins get an easy plug for your parts. Its nickname is BoB.

  1. micro:bit slot: push the micro:bit in here.
  2. Plugs for pins 0 to 8: for your parts.
  3. Plugs for pins 9 to 16: more places for your parts.
  4. Screen plug: the OLED display goes here.
  5. Extra pins: for add-ons you may try later.
  6. Power switch for pins 9 to 16: leave it on 3.3V unless your teacher says otherwise.

Why are the pins coloured?

The colours are a code so you don't mix things up. Yellow or blue = Signal (S): the message. Red = power (V; printed 3V3 or VCC on the board). Black = Ground (G): the way back. Plug each cable in with its black wire on the black row. The yellow group (pins 0 to 8) always gives 3.3V. The blue group (pins 9 to 16) gets its power from the little switch, so keep it on 3.3V.

What are the extra pins?

Some parts send lots of information, not just on or off. Chips can chat using shared rules, like a language. The black sockets at the bottom of the board are for two of these "languages": I2C (the screen plug, which the OLED display uses) and SPI (for add-ons you may try later). The pins on the sides are for add-ons too. You don't need any of these for your first projects.

Teacher notes

  • Compass: the first time you use it, the micro:bit asks you to tilt it until every light is on (it scrolls TILT TO FILL SCREEN). That is calibration, not a fault.
  • Temperature: the micro:bit reads its own chip, so the number is only a rough guide to the room.
  • Radio and Bluetooth: they cannot be used in the same program. MakeCode removes the radio blocks if you add the Bluetooth extension.
  • Shared pins: pins 3, 4, 6, 7 and 10 are shared with the LED display (and pin 9 on a V1), and pins 5 and 11 with buttons A and B. Avoid them while the lights or buttons are in use.
  • Breakout board switch: it sets the power for pins 9 to 16 (3.3V or 5V). Keep it on 3.3V; the micro:bit pins themselves only handle 3.3V.
  • GND is not earthing: the micro:bit's GND is only the return path of the circuit. It is not the safety earth in mains wiring, which gives fault current a safe route into the ground instead of through a person. The micro:bit's 3V is too low to be a shock risk.
